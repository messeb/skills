# Plain language (Plain English, Einfache Sprache, Leichte Sprache)

Goal of this skill: choose the right language level for the audience and the legal context, and apply its actual rules — rather than "writing simply" as an unspecified aspiration.

Use this skill for public-sector content, health and safety information, terms and conditions, onboarding, or any content with a legal accessibility obligation.

---

## 1. Plain language is a defined discipline, not a vibe

The working definition, consistent with **ISO 24495-1**: a communication is in plain language if its intended readers can **find** what they need, **understand** it, and **use** it — the first time they read it.

Three things follow. The standard is defined by the **reader's success**, not by the writer's judgement; it covers **structure and findability**, not only wording; and it is therefore testable — you can watch someone try to use the document.

Plain language is not: shorter at any cost, childish, imprecise, or free of technical terms. A tax form written in plain language still uses the correct legal term — it introduces it, uses it consistently, and does not surround it with unnecessary complexity.

---

## 2. The German three-level model

German makes a distinction English often blurs, and the levels are genuinely different products:

| Level | Audience | Character |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| **Fachsprache** | Specialists | Precise, dense, assumes shared vocabulary |
| **Standardsprache** | General adult readers | Ordinary careful writing |
| **Einfache Sprache** | Readers with limited reading practice, non-native speakers, anyone under time pressure | Simplified but still normal-looking German; roughly CEFR B1–A2 |
| **Leichte Sprache** | People with cognitive disabilities, learning difficulties, very low literacy | A **rule-governed variety** with its own strict conventions; roughly CEFR A1 |

The gap between the last two is the one that gets confused. **Einfache Sprache** is a style you apply. **Leichte Sprache** is a formalised set of rules with a distinctive appearance, its own layout conventions, and — done properly — a validation step by people from the target group.

---

## 3. Einfache Sprache — the working rules

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| Sentence length | One main idea per sentence; aim for roughly 15 words or fewer |
| Sentence structure | Main clauses; avoid nested subordinate clauses (`german-writing`) |
| Voice | Active; name who does what |
| Verbs over nouns | `Sie können beantragen` rather than `die Beantragung ist möglich` |
| Vocabulary | Everyday words; explain any term you must keep |
| Compounds | Split or hyphenate very long compounds where it aids reading |
| Abbreviations | Spell out on first use; avoid entirely where possible |
| Negation | Prefer positive statements; never double negatives |
| Figurative language | Avoid metaphor, irony, and idiom |
| Address | Speak to the reader directly (`Sie`) |
| Structure | Short paragraphs, meaningful headings, lists for steps |

Target roughly a German Flesch (Amstad) of 60–70 or a WSTF grade around 6–9 (`readability`) — measured after rewriting, not as the objective.

---

## 4. Leichte Sprache — the formal rules

Stricter, and visibly different. The established conventions include:

| Rule | Detail |
|------|--------|
| One sentence per line | Line breaks follow sense units, not the page width |
| Very short sentences | One statement per sentence |
| No subordinate clauses | Split into separate sentences instead |
| Active voice only | Passive is avoided entirely |
| No genitive | Rephrase with `von` |
| No subjunctive | State things directly |
| Compound words separated | Commonly with a mid-dot or hyphen (`Bundes-Kanzlerin`) |
| Concrete, everyday words | No abstractions, no foreign words unless explained |
| Explain every difficult word | On first use, in its own sentence |
| No metaphors or irony | Taken literally by many readers |
| Numbers and dates simplified | Arabic numerals; avoid large or precise figures where possible |
| Layout | Large type (typically ≥ 14 pt), left-aligned, generous spacing, supporting images |
| Validation | Reviewed by people from the target group before publication |

That last row is the one most often skipped, and it is the one that distinguishes genuine Leichte Sprache from a shortened text with big type. **Text that has not been validated by the target group should not be labelled Leichte Sprache.**

Practical note: Leichte Sprache is usually an **additional** version, not a replacement. Publish the standard version and link a Leichte-Sprache version alongside it.

## 5. Plain English

The same discipline, without the formalised level split. The core moves:

- Address the reader as **you**; name the actor (`we`, the agency, the company).
- **Active voice** by default; passive only when the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant.
- **Verbs over nominalisations**: `decide` rather than `make a determination`.
- **Everyday words**: `use` not `utilise`, `about` not `with regard to`, `if` not `in the event that`.
- **One idea per sentence**, average around 15–20 words with real variation.
- **Lists for steps and conditions** rather than a sentence with three semicolons.
- **Answer the reader's question first**, then explain (`text-structure`).
- **Define a term once**, then use it consistently — never vary it for elegance.

Regulatory context: public-sector plain-language requirements exist in several jurisdictions, and ISO 24495-1 gives an international reference you can cite in a style guide.

## 6. Accessibility obligations

Plain language sits inside a broader legal accessibility picture, particularly in the EU and Germany:

| Instrument | Relevance |
|-----------|-----------|
| **European Accessibility Act**, in Germany the **BFSG** (in force since June 2025) | Extends accessibility duties to many private-sector products and services, including e-commerce and consumer-facing digital services |
| **BITV 2.0** | German federal public-sector web accessibility; requires information about the site in **Leichte Sprache** and German Sign Language |
| **WCAG 2.2** | The technical baseline; readability sits under the *Understandable* principle |
| **EN 301 549** | The European harmonised standard referencing WCAG |

Two practical consequences. Public-sector German websites are generally expected to provide core information in Leichte Sprache. And accessibility is not only markup: a page that is technically perfect for a screen reader but written in impenetrable Behördendeutsch has not achieved the goal — though it may still satisfy the letter of a technical checklist.

Treat the legal position as something to confirm with counsel for your specific product; the point here is that language level can be a compliance question, not only an editorial preference.

## 7. Choosing and documenting a level

Decide deliberately and write the decision down:

1. **Who must succeed with this text?** Name the group, including non-native speakers and people under stress — an emergency instruction is read by people in a poor state to read.
2. **What must they be able to do?** Plain language is judged against a task.
3. **What is the legal context?** Public sector, consumer contract, health, safety.
4. **Which level?** Standard, Einfache Sprache, Leichte Sprache — or standard plus an additional simplified version.
5. **Which measure?** Name the index and target (`readability`).
6. **How will it be validated?** Reader testing; target-group validation for Leichte Sprache.

Record this in the style guide so that "make it simpler" becomes a specification instead of an opinion.

## 8. What plain language does not mean

| Misconception | Reality |
|---------------|---------|
| "No technical terms" | Keep the correct term; introduce it once in plain words |
| "Short sentences everywhere" | Uniformly short sentences read as staccato; vary length around a shorter average |
| "Dumbing down" | Experts read plain language faster too; nobody prefers difficulty |
| "Less precise" | Plain language is often **more** precise, because vagueness usually hides in complex constructions |
| "Just run it through a readability tool" | The tool measures surface features, not reader success |
| "One version fits everyone" | Different audiences may genuinely need different versions |

The precision point deserves emphasis: an impenetrable clause is frequently impenetrable because the writer had not decided what it meant. Rewriting it plainly forces the ambiguity to the surface — which is why legal review of a plain-language rewrite so often produces substantive questions rather than stylistic ones.

## 9. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Consequence |
|--------------|-------------|
| "Leichte Sprache" label on text never validated by the target group | Misleading claim; the readers it names still cannot use it |
| Einfache and Leichte Sprache treated as the same thing | Wrong rule set applied; effort wasted |
| Removing the technical term instead of explaining it | Ambiguity, and readers cannot connect the text to the form they are filling in |
| Simplifying wording while leaving the structure unchanged | The document is still unfindable |
| Publishing only a simplified version | Specialists lose the precise reference |
| Uniformly short sentences | Choppy, patronising, harder to follow |
| Plain-language rewrite without legal review for regulated content | Meaning changed inadvertently |
| Treating accessibility as markup only | Compliant code, incomprehensible content |
| Level chosen implicitly by whoever writes | Inconsistent voice across the product |
| Readability score used as proof of plain language | Surface metric substituted for reader success |

---

## 10. Checklist

- [ ] Audience named, including non-native speakers and readers under stress
- [ ] The task the reader must complete is stated
- [ ] Legal or accessibility obligations checked (BFSG, BITV, WCAG, sector rules)
- [ ] Language level chosen explicitly and recorded in the style guide
- [ ] Einfache Sprache and Leichte Sprache not conflated
- [ ] Leichte Sprache follows its full rule set, including layout
- [ ] Leichte Sprache validated by people from the target group before publication
- [ ] Simplified version published alongside the standard version, not instead of it
- [ ] Technical terms kept, introduced once, and used consistently
- [ ] Structure and findability addressed, not only wording
- [ ] Sentence length varied around a shorter average, not uniformly short
- [ ] Regulated content reviewed for meaning after the rewrite
- [ ] Index and target named; score used as confirmation, not as the goal
- [ ] Text tested with real readers attempting the real task
