# Social media and community

Goal of this skill: earn attention where your audience already spends it, and convert some of it into an owned relationship — rather than accumulating followers on a platform that can change the rules at any time.

Use this skill when choosing organic channels, when social output is high and results are flat, or when deciding whether to build a community.

---

## 1. Choose platforms by audience, not by trend

Two questions decide it: **is the audience there in meaningful numbers**, and **can we sustainably produce what performs there**? A platform that fits the first and not the second is a slow failure.

| Platform type | Fits | Format that works |
|---------------|------|-------------------|
| Professional network | B2B, recruiting, expertise-led | Text posts with a strong opening line, first-hand experience, documents |
| Short-form video | Broad consumer reach, demonstrable products | Hook in the first two seconds, native editing, unpolished |
| Image-led | Visual products, lifestyle, community | Consistent visual identity, saves and shares over likes |
| Real-time text | Developers, media, niche professional communities | Conversation, threads, being genuinely present |
| Video (long form) | Education, complex products, search-adjacent | Search-driven topics, watch time |
| Forums and Q&A | High-intent problem solving | Genuinely useful answers, no promotion |
| Chat communities | Practitioners, power users | Participation over broadcasting |

Two or three platforms done well beat six done badly. Sustainable output frequency matters more than platform choice — an abandoned account is worse than no account.

The honest constraint on all of it: **organic reach on major platforms is limited and declining**, algorithms change without notice, and the follower relationship belongs to the platform. Treat social as a discovery layer whose purpose is to move people to something you own — an email list, a community, an account.

## 2. Engagement triggers that work

Rather than a taxonomy of tricks, these are the motivations that reliably produce engagement. Each is legitimate when the underlying thing is real:

| Trigger | What it does | Fails when |
|---------|--------------|-----------|
| **Usefulness** | Teach something immediately applicable | It is thinly disguised promotion |
| **Identity** | Let people signal who they are by sharing it | It is generic |
| **Novelty** | Something genuinely new or counter-intuitive | Manufactured for the sake of it |
| **Recognition** | Feature customers, contributors, or the community | Transparently transactional |
| **Participation** | Ask a real question, run a poll, co-create | The question is rhetorical |
| **Generosity** | Give away something valuable — a template, data, access | Gated behind a form nobody wants |
| **Behind the scenes** | Show the real process, including failures | Sanitised into a press release |
| **Timeliness** | React to something the audience is already discussing | Forced relevance; hijacking something inappropriate |
| **Strong opinion** | Take a defensible position | Manufactured outrage |
| **Emotion** | Delight, surprise, humour | It does not match the brand |

Two cautions. **Reacting to current events** is effective and carries real risk: hijacking a serious news event, a tragedy, or a social movement for promotion produces a durable reputational cost that no engagement number offsets. And **manufactured controversy** works once and is remembered afterwards.

Format rules that hold everywhere: create **natively** for each platform rather than cross-posting one asset; the **first line or first two seconds** decide the outcome; platforms suppress content that sends people away, so lead with the value in-platform; respond to comments, because engagement in the first hour drives distribution; and post at a sustainable frequency rather than in bursts.

## 3. Employees and creators

**Employee advocacy** is usually a company's largest untapped organic reach: individual accounts reach further than brand accounts on professional networks, and carry more credibility. It works when people post in their own voice about their own work, and fails immediately when it becomes mandated sharing of corporate posts. Support it with material, not scripts.

**Creator collaboration** is covered in `referral`; the relevant point here is that relevance and engagement beat follower count, that creative freedom outperforms scripts, and that **disclosure is a legal requirement** in the EU for any incentivised content (`growth-legal-and-ethics`).

## 4. Community: owned versus rented

| | Participating in existing communities | Building your own |
|---|---|---|
| Cost to start | Low | High |
| Time to value | Weeks | Many months |
| Control | None | Full |
| Risk | Being seen as an outsider marketer | Building an empty room |
| Value | Reach, research, credibility | Retention, support deflection, advocacy, product feedback |

Participate first. Build your own only when there is a genuine reason for members to talk **to each other** — not merely to you. A community whose only value is company announcements is a mailing list with extra steps.

If you build one: seed it with a small number of genuinely engaged members before opening it; give it a clear purpose and rules; ensure someone owns it as a real job; make it easy to get value on the first visit; and measure health (active participants, questions answered, member-to-member interaction) rather than member count.

Communities are a strong **retention** mechanism and a slow acquisition one. Justify them primarily on retention, support deflection, and product feedback (`retention`).

Participating in communities you do not own has one rule that matters: **be a participant, not an advertiser**. Answer questions where you have no commercial interest, disclose your affiliation when you do, and follow the community's promotion rules exactly. The reputational cost of getting this wrong outlives the campaign (`idea-generation`).

## 5. Social listening

Social and community are a research channel before they are a distribution one:

- **Objections and language** — how people describe the problem, in their words, becomes ad copy and page headlines (`customer-research`).
- **Competitor complaints** — the most direct source of differentiation and comparison-content topics.
- **Unmet needs** — feature requests and workarounds discussed publicly.
- **Brand mentions** — including misspellings and mentions without tags, which are the ones nobody sees.
- **Support signals** — questions asked publicly instead of in your support channel.

Set up monitoring for your brand, your competitors, and two or three problem phrases. This is one of the cheapest sources of experiment ideas available.

## 6. Measurement

| Level | Metric | Note |
|-------|--------|------|
| Vanity | Followers, likes, impressions | Report only as context; never as the goal |
| Engagement | Comments, saves, shares, replies | Saves and shares indicate value better than likes |
| Traffic | Clicks to owned properties | Platforms suppress outbound links; expect low rates |
| **Capture** | Email signups, community joins, trials | The metric that matters — moving rented attention to owned |
| Pipeline | Qualified signups and revenue attributed | Use self-reported attribution; last-click will show near zero |
| Health (community) | Active members, questions answered, member-to-member ratio | Better than member count |

Social attribution is genuinely poor — it is a discovery touch that last-click models will value at almost nothing. Rely on **self-reported attribution** in the signup flow and on trend correlation, and accept directional evidence rather than manufacturing precision (`acquisition`).

## 7. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Consequence |
|--------------|-------------|
| Being on six platforms because competitors are | Thin, abandoned accounts on all of them |
| Cross-posting one asset everywhere | Underperforms on every platform |
| Broadcasting without responding | Algorithms suppress it; the audience disengages |
| Follower count as the objective | Reach that never converts |
| Never moving audience to an owned channel | The relationship stays rented and revocable |
| Buying followers or engagement | Detectable, worthless, and a platform-terms violation |
| Mandated employee sharing of corporate posts | Visibly inauthentic; reach falls |
| Marketing in communities as an outsider | Reputational damage outliving the campaign |
| Newsjacking a tragedy or a social movement | Durable brand damage for a spike |
| Manufactured controversy | Works once; remembered afterwards |
| Building a community with no member-to-member reason to exist | An empty room requiring permanent staffing |
| Community measured on member count | Health and value invisible |
| Undisclosed paid creator content | Legal liability for brand and creator |
| Social judged on last-click attribution | Systematically undervalued and cut |

## 8. Checklist

- [ ] Platforms chosen on audience presence and sustainable output capacity
- [ ] Two or three platforms committed to, rather than six
- [ ] Content created natively per platform, not cross-posted
- [ ] Opening line or first two seconds treated as the primary lever
- [ ] Value delivered in-platform, with a defined route to an owned channel
- [ ] Comments answered, especially in the first hour
- [ ] Employee advocacy supported with material, never mandated scripts
- [ ] Creator collaborations disclosed as advertising
- [ ] Existing communities joined as a participant, with affiliation disclosed
- [ ] Own community justified on retention and feedback, with an owner and a purpose
- [ ] Community health measured, not member count
- [ ] Social listening set up for brand, competitors, and problem phrases
- [ ] Listening insights fed into the experiment backlog and ad copy
- [ ] Capture metric (email, community, trial) treated as the goal, not followers
- [ ] Self-reported attribution used; last-click limitations acknowledged
