# Growth strategy

Goal of this skill: decide **where** growth is supposed to come from, so that experiments test a thesis rather than wander through tactics.

Use this skill at the start of a growth programme, when planning a year or a quarter, when entering a market, or when a team is running many experiments with no cumulative direction.

---

## 1. The first-mover myth

"Get there first" is weak strategy and often wrong. First movers pay to educate a market, build infrastructure that later entrants use cheaply, and make the category's early mistakes. Most durable category leaders arrived second or later with a better execution of a validated idea.

What actually creates defensibility:

| Advantage | Why it holds |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Compounding loops** | Each cohort makes acquisition cheaper for the next (`growth-loops`) |
| **Network effects** | Value rises with participants; leaving costs the user something |
| **Switching costs** | Data, integrations, workflow, and habit |
| **Proprietary data** | The product improves in ways competitors cannot copy |
| **Brand and trust** | Cheapest acquisition channel there is, and the slowest to build |
| **Unit-economics advantage** | You can profitably pay more per customer than competitors can |

Being early only matters when it is converted into one of these. Ask directly: *if a well-funded competitor copied our product tomorrow, what would still be hard?* If the answer is "nothing", growth spend buys a rented position.

## 2. A lean growth strategy on one page

Keep it short enough that the team can recite it. Every experiment should be traceable to a line on it.

```markdown
# Growth strategy — <period>

**Where we play**: <segment, geography, use case — and explicitly who we are not for>
**How we win**: <the one thing we are better at, in the customer's words>
**Positioning**: For <segment> who <situation>, we are the <category> that <differentiator>, unlike <alternative>.

**North star**: <metric> — from <baseline> to <target> by <date>   (see north-star-and-metrics)

**Growth model**: <the loop or engine we believe compounds>
**Primary constraint this period**: <acquisition | activation | retention | referral | revenue>

**Bets** (3 at most)
| # | Bet | Why we believe it | How we will know within <n> weeks | Owner |
|---|-----|-------------------|-----------------------------------|-------|

**Not doing** (and why): <the channels, segments, and features deliberately excluded>
```

Two disciplines make this useful. **Name the constraint**: at any moment one stage of the funnel limits growth, and effort spent elsewhere is largely wasted — pouring traffic into a product that does not retain is the classic error. **Write the "not doing" list**: a strategy that excludes nothing decides nothing.

## 3. Positioning as a growth constraint

Positioning is not a branding exercise that happens after the product; it determines your conversion rate, your acquisition cost, and which channels can possibly work.

Symptoms of bad positioning showing up as a growth problem: high traffic and low conversion; sales cycles dominated by "how are you different from X?"; paid channels that never reach payback because the click has to do the explaining; and content that ranks but does not convert.

The test: can a prospect, after five seconds on the page, say **what it is, who it is for, and what it replaces?** If not, no channel experiment will fix the funnel.

Brand and growth are not opposites. Brand is the mechanism by which acquisition gets cheaper over time — direct traffic, branded search, higher click-through on the same creative, and better conversion at the same price. Treat brand investment as a long-horizon growth channel with lagging measurement, not as a cost centre exempt from measurement.

## 4. Competitor and market analysis

The goal is not imitation. It is **finding where demand already exists and what it currently costs to reach it**, so you know which channels are proven, which are saturated, and where a gap sits.

| Question | How to answer it |
|----------|------------------|
| Which channels do they actually rely on? | Traffic-mix estimates from a market intelligence tool; check whether paid, organic, or referral dominates |
| What are they spending on ads, and where? | Ad transparency libraries (Meta, TikTok, LinkedIn, Google) show live creatives; paid-keyword tools estimate spend |
| Which creatives and offers do they keep running? | Ads running unchanged for months are ads that work — longevity is the signal, not cleverness |
| Which keywords do they buy and rank for? | Keyword gap analysis reveals demand they capture and terms nobody owns |
| Which content earns their links? | Backlink analysis shows what the market cites |
| How is their funnel built? | Sign up as a customer and document the entire flow — onboarding emails, paywall, upgrade prompts, cancellation flow |
| What do their customers complain about? | Reviews, support forums, app store one-star reviews, social mentions |
| How do they price and package? | Pricing page history via a web archive shows what they tested and kept |

Two techniques worth naming because they are cheap and disproportionately informative:

- **Become a customer of your competitor.** Sign up with a fresh address, buy something, then cancel. You will see the full lifecycle — the emails, the offers, the retention save, the dark patterns — that no analytics tool can show you. Keep the artefacts.
- **Watch what they keep, not what they launch.** Launches are noise; a campaign, ad, or landing page still running after three months has passed their measurement, and that is a free result.

Handle this properly: use public sources and your own account, respect terms of service, do not scrape what you are not permitted to scrape, do not misrepresent your identity to obtain confidential information, and never use a competitor's customer data. Competitive intelligence is a legitimate discipline; industrial espionage is a crime (`growth-legal-and-ethics`).

Note the limit of tool-based estimates: third-party traffic and spend figures are modelled, often wrong by a large factor, and best used for **relative comparison and trend**, not as absolute numbers in a business case.

## 5. Choosing where to compete

| Situation | Strategic move |
|-----------|----------------|
| Large incumbent, saturated head terms | Win a specific segment or use case completely before broadening |
| Demand exists, no one owns the category language | Content and category positioning; own the search demand |
| Channel is expensive for everyone | Find a channel with a structural advantage for your product, or build a loop that lowers CAC |
| Product is genuinely differentiated but unknown | Reduce the cost of understanding: demos, free tiers, comparison content |
| Undifferentiated product | Stop; a growth programme cannot fix an undifferentiated offer — it only raises the cost of discovering that |

The last row is the one teams skip. If retention and word of mouth are both weak, the constraint is the product, and every euro of acquisition spend makes the problem more expensive.

## 6. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Consequence |
|--------------|-------------|
| Experiments with no strategic thesis | Motion without cumulative learning |
| Racing to be first without a defensibility plan | Educating a market for a competitor |
| Strategy that excludes nothing | Resources spread across everything, nothing wins |
| Ignoring the current constraint | Effort applied where it cannot move the outcome |
| Positioning treated as a later branding project | Every channel underperforms and nobody knows why |
| Copying a competitor's channel mix | You inherit their constraints without their assets |
| Modelled traffic estimates used as absolute figures in a business case | Plans built on numbers that are wrong by multiples |
| Brand treated as unmeasurable and therefore unaccountable | Spend without a payback thesis |
| Growth spend on an undifferentiated product | Expensive confirmation that positioning is the problem |
| Competitive research that breaks terms of service or the law | Legal exposure, reputational damage |

---

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] One-page strategy written, with "where we play" and "how we win"
- [ ] Positioning statement passes the five-second test on the live site
- [ ] North star metric and target agreed for the period
- [ ] The current growth constraint named explicitly
- [ ] At most three strategic bets, each with a falsification window and an owner
- [ ] "Not doing" list written
- [ ] Defensibility identified: what stays hard if a competitor copies the product
- [ ] Competitor channel mix, ad creatives, keywords, and funnel documented from public sources
- [ ] Long-running competitor campaigns identified as proven-offer evidence
- [ ] Competitor lifecycle experienced first-hand and archived
- [ ] Third-party estimates used comparatively, never as absolutes
- [ ] Competitive research reviewed against terms of service and law
- [ ] Every planned experiment traceable to a bet on the strategy page
