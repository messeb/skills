# Growth hacking fundamentals

Goal of this skill: a working definition of growth hacking that a team can act on, and an honest account of where it helps, where it does not, and how it goes wrong.

Use this skill when introducing growth practice to a team, when "growth hacking" is being used to mean "cheap marketing tricks", or when deciding whether the approach fits your organisation at all.

---

## 1. What "growth" means before you hack it

"Growth" is not traffic, followers, or signups. It is a **sustained increase in the value a business creates and captures** — usually expressed as more customers who stay longer and pay more, at a cost of acquisition the business can afford.

The reason everyone wants it: growth compounds, and it is the only thing that reliably converts a good product into a durable business. The reason it is so often faked: vanity metrics rise easily. Registrations, impressions, and app installs can all be bought; retained paying customers cannot.

A useful test for any growth claim: **would this still look like growth if the metric were revenue retained after 90 days?**

---

## 2. Definition

> Growth hacking is a **cross-functional, experiment-driven practice** that uses the whole product — not only marketing — to find repeatable, scalable levers for growth, and validates each one with data before scaling it.

Four load-bearing parts of that sentence:

| Part | Why it matters |
|------|----------------|
| **Cross-functional** | Levers sit in product, engineering, data, support and pricing, not just in campaigns |
| **Experiment-driven** | Ideas are hypotheses with defined success criteria, not decisions |
| **The whole product** | The strongest levers are usually built into the product (sharing, onboarding, referral), not bolted on |
| **Validated before scaling** | Spending follows evidence; that is what separates it from campaign marketing |

What it is **not**: a synonym for "cheap", a list of tricks, a substitute for a product people want, or a marketing job title with a fashionable name.

---

## 3. It is not only for startups

The practice is associated with startups because startups have no budget and no brand to protect, so experimentation is the only option. But the method transfers, with adjustments:

| Context | What changes |
|---------|--------------|
| **Startup** | Speed and channel discovery dominate; almost nothing is off-limits; the bottleneck is finding product-market fit |
| **Scale-up** | Loops and retention dominate; the bottleneck is making acquisition repeatable and efficient |
| **Established company** | Brand risk, legal review, and existing traffic change the calculus — but existing traffic also makes experiments statistically viable in days rather than months |
| **B2B** | Long cycles, buying committees, small populations |
| **Regulated industries** | Legal review is part of the experiment design, not an afterthought (`growth-legal-and-ethics`) |

### B2B specifically

B2B growth work is real, but three constraints reshape it:

- **Small populations.** With 400 target accounts you cannot run a conventional A/B test to significance. Use qualitative validation, sequential testing, and holdout groups over longer windows — and accept directional evidence honestly labelled as such.
- **Long sales cycles.** The feedback loop between experiment and revenue can be months. Instrument leading indicators (meeting booked, pilot started, security review passed) and validate that they predict the lagging one.
- **Buying committees.** The user, the champion, the budget holder, and the blocker are different people with different objections. "The customer" is a group.

The established-company advantage nobody exploits: existing traffic and existing customers make experiments cheap and fast. A company with 50,000 monthly visitors can settle in a week a question a startup cannot answer in a quarter.

## 4. Does it save money?

Partly — and it is the wrong first question.

It saves money by **failing cheaply**: a smoke test costs a few hundred euros and can prevent a six-month build. It saves money by killing channels that do not pay back before the budget scales into them.

It costs money in **people and instrumentation**: analytics that can be trusted, a team with time to run experiments, and the discipline to run losing tests. A team that runs ten experiments and scales the two winners has spent real money on the eight losers — that spend is the price of the evidence, not waste.

The honest framing: growth practice improves the **return on marketing and product spend**, and reduces the size of the bets you lose. It is not a way to grow without spending.

## 5. When is a hack a hack?

Distinguish three things that get called the same name:

| | Definition | Lifespan |
|---|---|---|
| **A trick** | Exploits a loophole in a platform's rules or a user's inattention | Short — closes, or damages trust |
| **A hack** | An unconventional, disproportionately effective use of an existing system, within its rules | Medium — works until copied or the platform changes |
| **A lever** | A structural property of the product that produces growth repeatedly | Long — compounds (`growth-loops`) |

A hack qualifies when it has: an **asymmetric payoff** (small effort, large result), a **non-obvious mechanism** (someone had to notice it), and a **measurable effect** you can prove.

Practical guidance: hacks buy time; **loops build businesses**. A team whose entire growth plan is a list of hacks has no plan, because every hack decays. Use hacks to find where the leverage is, then build the lever into the product.

Two warnings worth stating plainly. First, anything that depends on violating a platform's terms of service is a liability, not a strategy — accounts get banned, and the loss usually lands after you have become dependent on the channel. Second, a tactic that works by misleading users is not a hack; it borrows from trust and repays with churn (`behavioral-psychology`, `growth-legal-and-ethics`).

## 6. The five pillars

### Growth mindset

Treat the current numbers as a starting point, not a verdict. Concretely: ask "what would have to be true for this to grow 10×?" before asking "how do we improve this by 5%?"; expect most experiments to fail and design so failure is cheap; and treat a disproved hypothesis as a result, not an embarrassment.

### Facts over opinions

The most senior opinion in the room is still an opinion. Rules that operationalise this: every claim about users names its evidence; disagreements convert into experiments rather than escalations; the person who proposed the idea does not get to reinterpret a losing result; and instrumentation comes before the experiment, not after.

### Customer understanding

You cannot hack growth for a customer you cannot describe. This means their situation, their alternative, the moment they decide, and the language they use — not demographics (`customer-research`).

### Channel diversity

Most companies get most of their growth from one or two channels, and most have never seriously tested more than three. Systematic breadth before depth is what finds the channel that works (`idea-generation`, `acquisition`).

### Optimization

Finding a channel is the beginning. Compounding comes from repeated improvement of conversion, retention, and unit economics along the whole funnel — the unglamorous part where most of the durable gain actually lives.

## 7. Compared with adjacent models

| Model | Relationship to growth hacking |
|-------|-------------------------------|
| **Lean Startup** | The parent methodology — build-measure-learn applied to the whole business. Growth practice is the operational version focused on growth metrics |
| **Digital marketing** | Overlaps on channels, differs in scope: growth work changes the product, pricing, and onboarding, not only the campaign |
| **CRO** | A subset — activation and conversion optimization is one stage of the funnel |
| **Product management** | Shares the discovery toolkit; growth work optimises an existing value proposition rather than defining it |
| **Performance marketing** | Shares measurement rigour; growth work is not limited to paid channels and owns retention |
| **Data science** | Provides the measurement; growth practice provides the questions and the interventions |

The honest answer to "is this old wine in new bottles?": largely yes on the individual techniques — direct marketers have been testing offers for a century. What is genuinely new is the **combination**: product engineering inside the growth loop, near-zero-cost experimentation on digital channels, and instrumentation that makes causal claims testable within days.

## 8. The dangers

| Danger | What it looks like | Guard |
|--------|--------------------|-------|
| **Growth before product-market fit** | Pouring acquisition into a product people leave | Measure retention first (`product-market-fit`) |
| **Vanity metrics** | Impressions and signups reported as growth | One north star tied to value delivered (`north-star-and-metrics`) |
| **Local optimisation** | Twelve button tests, no structural change | Balance the portfolio between small and structural bets |
| **Short-termism** | Discounting and pressure tactics that lift this quarter and depress next year | Track retention and margin alongside conversion |
| **Trust erosion** | Manipulative patterns, spam, deceptive claims | Ethical limits agreed in advance (`growth-legal-and-ethics`) |
| **Legal exposure** | Scraping, unsolicited email, undisclosed advertising, sweepstakes rules | Legal review inside the experiment template |
| **Platform dependence** | One channel, one algorithm change away from zero | Channel diversity as an explicit portfolio target |
| **Experiment theatre** | Many tests, no decisions, no learning | Every experiment ends in ship / kill / iterate, written down |
| **Get-rich-quick framing** | The tactic list is treated as the strategy | Hacks find leverage; loops and retention keep it |

---

## 9. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Consequence |
|--------------|-------------|
| "Growth hacking" used to mean "marketing without a budget" | The product levers, where the real leverage is, are never touched |
| Copying a famous company's hack without its context | The mechanism depended on a platform, an era, or an audience you do not have |
| A single growth person with no engineering access | Can only run campaigns; cannot change the product |
| Experiments with no pre-declared success criterion | Results are reinterpreted until they are positive |
| Scaling spend on a channel before payback is proven | Loss discovered at volume instead of at test size |
| Treating the tactic list as the strategy | Every tactic decays; nothing compounds |
| Ignoring retention because acquisition is more visible | A leaking bucket filled faster |
| Copying tactics that require breaking platform rules | Account loss after you depend on the channel |

---

## 10. Checklist

- [ ] "Growth" defined for this business in terms of retained value, not vanity metrics
- [ ] Product-market fit assessed before scaling acquisition
- [ ] Cross-functional access secured: product, engineering, data, marketing, support
- [ ] Growth practice adapted to context (startup / scale-up / established / B2B / regulated)
- [ ] B2B: leading indicators defined and validated against the lagging revenue metric
- [ ] One north star metric agreed and tied to delivered value
- [ ] Evidence standard agreed: every user claim names its source
- [ ] Experiment portfolio balanced between optimisation and structural bets
- [ ] Channel portfolio target set; dependence on any single channel tracked
- [ ] Ethical and legal limits written down before the first experiment
- [ ] Hacks understood as time-buying; at least one compounding loop identified
- [ ] Every experiment ends in a written decision: ship, kill, or iterate
