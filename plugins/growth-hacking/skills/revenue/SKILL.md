# Revenue

Goal of this skill: capture more of the value you create — through model, pricing, packaging, and conversion — without buying short-term revenue at the cost of retention.

Use this skill when unit economics do not work, when free users never convert, when pricing has never been tested, or when growth is healthy and monetisation is not.

Pricing is the most under-tested lever in most companies. It requires no traffic, no build, and it moves revenue directly.

---

## 1. Choosing a model

| Model | Fits | Main risk |
|-------|------|-----------|
| **Subscription** | Recurring value, predictable use | Churn compounds against you |
| **Freemium** | Low marginal cost, viral or network potential, large market | Free tier cannibalises paid; support cost without revenue |
| **Free trial** | Value is obvious quickly | Trial length mismatched to time to value |
| **Usage-based** | Value scales with consumption | Unpredictable bills deter buyers; revenue is volatile |
| **Hybrid (base + usage)** | Predictability plus upside | Complexity in communication |
| **Transactional / e-commerce** | One-off purchases | Acquisition must be repaid on the first order |
| **Marketplace** | Two-sided value | Cold start; leakage off-platform |
| **Advertising** | Very large audiences, low willingness to pay | Needs scale; interests misaligned with users |
| **Affiliate** | Content assets with purchase intent | Dependent on someone else's programme and cookies |
| **White label / licensing** | Product usable under another brand | Channel conflict; long cycles |

Two structural questions decide most of it: **how often does the customer get value** (frequent → subscription; rare → transactional), and **can you serve a free user at near-zero marginal cost** (yes → freemium is possible; no → free trial instead). Products with real per-user costs — inference, storage, support — should be very careful with unlimited free tiers.

## 2. Pricing and packaging

Pricing is a research question, not a debate:

| Method | Produces |
|--------|----------|
| **Van Westendorp** | The acceptable price range from four questions |
| **Willingness-to-pay interviews** | The reference price and the alternative being compared |
| **Conjoint analysis** | The relative value of features and their price sensitivity |
| **A/B on new customers** | Real behaviour, the strongest evidence |
| **Competitor and alternative analysis** | The reference point in the buyer's head, including "a spreadsheet" |
| **Historical discount analysis** | What you already accept in practice, which is your real price |

**Value metric** — what you charge per — matters more than the number. A good one scales with the value the customer receives (seats, transactions, contacts, volume), is easy to predict and explain, and does not punish the behaviour you want. A value metric that penalises adoption (charging per document in a product whose value grows with documents) suppresses the usage that creates retention.

Packaging rules that hold across most B2B and prosumer products: three tiers is the practical default; make the middle one the intended choice and design the others around it; differentiate on **value-related** dimensions (volume, seats, capability) rather than randomly withheld features; and put the price on the website — hiding it filters out more good-fit buyers than it protects margin, except in genuine enterprise sales.

For A/B testing price: test on **new customers only**, keep existing customers on their terms, run whole billing cycles, and measure revenue per visitor together with retention — not just conversion rate. A cheaper price that converts more and retains worse is a loss that takes two quarters to appear.

## 3. The freemium problem

Freemium fails in a specific way: the free tier is generous enough that nobody needs to upgrade, and support costs accumulate against zero revenue.

The design question is **what creates the upgrade need**, and it should be a natural consequence of success with the product:

| Limit type | Works when |
|------------|-----------|
| **Volume** (records, projects, messages) | Usage grows naturally with value |
| **Seats** | Value is collaborative |
| **Capability** (advanced or admin features) | Serious users need them; casual users do not |
| **Support level** | Business customers value response times |
| **Time** (trial) | Value is obvious within the window |

Principles: the free tier must deliver **real, complete value** for its intended user — a crippled free tier produces neither advocates nor upgrades. The limit should be reached by **succeeding**, not by being blocked arbitrarily. Communicate approach to the limit early, so the upgrade is planned rather than an interruption. And measure the free tier's cost honestly: infrastructure, support, and the acquisition spent on users who will never pay.

Free is justified when it produces something of value: a loop, a network, content, distribution, or a pipeline of qualified upgrades. If it produces none of those, it is a cost centre with a marketing story attached.

## 4. Upgrade, expansion, and cross-sell

**Expansion revenue** from existing customers is cheaper than new acquisition and is the strongest retention signal there is (`retention`).

| Mechanic | Practice |
|----------|----------|
| **Contextual upgrade prompts** | At the moment the limit is reached or the capability is needed — not on a schedule |
| **Usage-based expansion** | Growth in seats or volume raises revenue automatically |
| **Cross-sell** | Offer the complementary product after success with the first, not during onboarding |
| **Bundles** | Price the bundle below the sum, and make the saving explicit |
| **Anchoring** | A higher tier shown alongside makes the intended tier look reasonable — legitimate when the tier is real |
| **Annual plans** | Discount for commitment improves cash and retention |
| **Order-value increases** | Free-shipping thresholds, quantity breaks, relevant add-ons at checkout |

Rules that keep this from degrading the experience: relevance beats frequency, and one well-timed prompt outperforms five generic ones; never interrupt the task the customer came to do; and cross-sell **after** value, never before — an upsell during onboarding costs activation, which costs more than the upsell earns.

## 5. Discount discipline

Discounts are the fastest way to raise this month's revenue and the most reliable way to damage next year's.

What goes wrong: customers learn to wait for the sale; the discounted cohort retains worse and churns at renewal when the real price appears; margin falls without a volume gain that compensates; and the discount becomes the reference price, making the list price uncredible.

Better instruments: an annual-commitment discount (buys cash and retention), a genuine time-limited launch price for a new product, volume or tier pricing, and value-add instead of price cuts (extra seats, onboarding support, an extended trial). If discounting is necessary, make it **conditional** — on commitment, on volume, on a case study — so something is received in return.

Always measure discounted cohorts separately. Their retention and expansion behaviour is usually worse, and blending them into the aggregate hides it.

## 6. Checkout and payment

The final steps are where funded, motivated demand is lost:

- Show the **total price early**, including tax and shipping. Surprise costs at the final step are the single largest cause of cart abandonment.
- Offer **guest checkout**; forced account creation costs conversion for no benefit.
- Support the **payment methods your market actually uses** — in the DACH region, invoice, SEPA direct debit, and PayPal materially outperform card-only checkouts.
- Keep the form minimal and correctly typed; support address autofill (`activation`).
- Show **trust signals near the payment step**: security marks, refund policy, cancellation terms, contact route.
- **Handle failures gracefully**: a declined card should keep the cart, explain what happened, and offer another method.
- **Dunning matters**: failed recurring payments are a large share of churn and are recovered with retries, card-update prompts, and clear notices (`retention`).
- Note the legal requirements in the EU: a clearly labelled order button (the German *Button-Lösung*), full price transparency including VAT, and correct withdrawal-right information (`growth-legal-and-ethics`).

## 7. Copywriting that sells honestly

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Benefit before feature** | State the outcome, then how it is achieved |
| **Specificity over superlatives** | "Cuts month-end close from 5 days to 1" beats "revolutionary" |
| **Customer's language** | Use the words from research and support logs, not internal vocabulary |
| **One idea per section** | Competing messages cancel out |
| **Answer the objection at the point of hesitation** | Cancellation terms next to the buy button, not in a FAQ |
| **Proof next to the claim** | A number, a customer, a screenshot |
| **Clear next step** | Every section ends knowing what to do |

The honesty constraint is also a commercial one: claims must be substantiated. Invented scarcity, fake countdowns, fabricated reviews, and "was/now" pricing where the "was" was never charged are not clever copywriting — they are misleading commercial practices, actionable in the EU, and they raise refunds and chargebacks even when nobody sues (`behavioral-psychology`, `growth-legal-and-ethics`).

## 8. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Consequence |
|--------------|-------------|
| Pricing set once and never tested | The highest-leverage lever left untouched |
| Value metric that penalises usage | Suppresses the behaviour that drives retention |
| Price tested on existing customers | Trust damage and support load |
| Conversion optimised without watching retention | Cheaper price, worse cohort, net loss |
| Free tier so generous nobody upgrades | Costs without revenue |
| Free tier so crippled it delivers no value | Neither advocates nor upgrades |
| Free tier cost never measured | An invisible, growing cost centre |
| Cross-sell during onboarding | Activation lost, which costs more than the upsell earns |
| Habitual discounting | Customers wait for sales; list price loses credibility |
| Discounted cohorts blended into aggregates | Worse retention hidden |
| Surprise costs at the final checkout step | The largest single cause of abandonment |
| Card-only checkout in markets that prefer other methods | Avoidable lost sales |
| Failed payments not worked | A large share of churn left uncollected |
| Fake scarcity or fabricated reviews | Refunds, chargebacks, and legal exposure |

---

## 9. Checklist

- [ ] Business model matched to value frequency and marginal cost per user
- [ ] Value metric scales with delivered value and does not punish adoption
- [ ] Pricing researched with at least one structured method, not decided by debate
- [ ] Price A/B tested on new customers only, over whole billing cycles
- [ ] Revenue per visitor and cohort retention measured together, not conversion alone
- [ ] Packaging differentiates on value-related dimensions; intended tier designed for
- [ ] Freemium limits reached by succeeding, not by arbitrary blocking
- [ ] Free-tier cost (infrastructure, support, acquisition) measured
- [ ] Upgrade prompts contextual and non-interruptive
- [ ] Cross-sell offered after value, never during onboarding
- [ ] Discounts conditional; discounted cohorts tracked separately
- [ ] Total price shown early; guest checkout available
- [ ] Local payment methods supported for the target market
- [ ] Payment failures handled with retries, card-update prompts, and clear notices
- [ ] Checkout meets local legal requirements (order button, price transparency, withdrawal rights)
- [ ] Every claim in the copy substantiated; no invented scarcity or fabricated proof
