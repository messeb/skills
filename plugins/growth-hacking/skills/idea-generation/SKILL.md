# Generating growth ideas

Goal of this skill: produce a large, diverse pool of candidate experiments on demand, so prioritisation has something worth ranking — and so channel choice is systematic rather than habitual.

Use this skill when the backlog is thin, when every idea is a variation of the last one, or when choosing which acquisition channels to test.

---

## 1. Channels first: the coverage problem

Most companies use two or three channels and have never seriously evaluated the rest. The traction-channel checklist exists to force breadth before depth:

| # | Channel | # | Channel |
|---|---------|---|---------|
| 1 | Search engine optimisation | 11 | Trade shows |
| 2 | Search engine marketing (paid search) | 12 | Offline events |
| 3 | Social and display advertising | 13 | Speaking engagements |
| 4 | Offline advertising | 14 | Community building |
| 5 | Content marketing / blogs | 15 | Affiliate programmes |
| 6 | Email marketing | 16 | Existing platforms (app stores, marketplaces, integrations) |
| 7 | Public relations | 17 | Business development and partnerships |
| 8 | Unconventional PR / stunts | 18 | Sales |
| 9 | Viral marketing | 19 | Engineering as marketing (free tools, calculators, APIs) |
| 10 | Targeted blogs and niche media | | |

How to use it: score **every** channel with BRASS (`experiment-prioritization`), pick the three or four most promising that you are **not** currently using, and design a cheap test for each. The point is not to run all nineteen; it is to make the choice deliberate and to notice the channel you have been ignoring because nobody on the team has done it before.

Two observations that repeatedly hold. **The channel that works is often not the obvious one** for your category — obvious channels are also the most competitive and therefore the most expensive. And **engineering as marketing is systematically under-used**: a free tool that solves a small adjacent problem is cheap to build, hard for competitors to copy, and produces content, links, and signups at once.

## 2. Mining your own data

Before running a workshop, extract the ideas already sitting in your systems. This is the highest-yield and least-used source.

| Source | What to look for |
|--------|------------------|
| Funnel analytics | The biggest drop-off — every step above 30% loss is an idea generator |
| Site search | What people look for and do not find; zero-result queries are a product roadmap |
| Support tickets and chat logs | The exact words customers use for their problems; recurring confusion |
| Sales call recordings | Objections, comparisons, the moment of decision |
| Cancellation reasons | Free, honest, and almost never read systematically |
| Session recordings and heatmaps | Rage clicks, dead clicks, form abandonment points |
| Search Console queries | Terms you already rank for but do not serve well |
| Reviews (yours and competitors') | One-star reviews describe unmet needs precisely |
| Cohort differences | What distinguishes retained users from churned ones in week one |
| Power-user behaviour | Which action correlates with retention — a candidate activation event |

The last two are the most valuable, because they point at the mechanism rather than the symptom.

## 3. Structured ideation methods

Unstructured brainstorming underperforms: the loudest voice anchors the room, and production drops as people wait their turn. Use a structure.

### Silent methods: brainwriting 6-3-5 and brainswarming

Six people, three ideas each, five rounds. Everyone writes three ideas on a sheet in five minutes, then passes it on; the next person builds on what they read. Thirty minutes produces up to 108 ideas, with no anchoring and no interruption.

Best for: volume and for teams where seniority otherwise dominates. Follow with clustering.

**Brainswarming** is the same principle applied to a shared surface rather than passed sheets. The goal goes at the top, available resources at the bottom, and everyone silently adds notes — breaking the goal into sub-goals downward, and combining resources into capabilities upward — until the two halves meet in the middle. Nobody speaks; the board is the conversation.

It fits growth work well because the two halves map directly onto the constraint (top) and the channels, assets, and audiences you already have (bottom). Where 6-3-5 maximises idea volume, brainswarming maximises the chance that an idea is actually feasible with what you have.

### The Walt Disney method

Three sequential roles, held by the whole group in turn — never simultaneously:

1. **Dreamer** — what would we do with unlimited resources? No objections permitted.
2. **Realist** — how could we actually do a version of that? Concrete steps and requirements.
3. **Critic** — what would go wrong? Risks, costs, legal, brand.

The value is the separation. Critique that arrives during ideation kills ideas before they are understood; critique that never arrives ships bad ones. Run the loop twice — the second dreamer pass, informed by the critic, is usually where the good idea appears.

### Osborn checklist / SCAMPER

Apply systematic transformations to something that already works — an existing page, offer, channel, or feature:

| Prompt | Applied to growth |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Substitute** | Different channel, offer, audience, format |
| **Combine** | Bundle two features; co-market with a partner |
| **Adapt** | Copy a mechanic from another industry |
| **Modify / magnify** | Ten times bigger, longer, more generous |
| **Put to other use** | Sell the same thing to a different segment |
| **Eliminate** | Remove a form field, a step, a plan, a price tier |
| **Reverse / rearrange** | Invert the funnel order; free at the end instead of the start |

"Eliminate" is the most productive prompt in practice, and the least used: removing a step is almost always cheaper and more effective than adding a persuasion tactic.

### Adjacent-industry mining

Take a mechanic that works in a completely different industry and transplant it: subscription boxes from retail into B2B; loyalty tiers from airlines into SaaS; matchmaking from dating into recruiting. Novelty in your category is a real advantage, because your audience has not been trained to ignore it.

## 4. Finding waterholes

A waterhole is a place your target audience already gathers. Finding them turns "reach our audience" into a list of concrete, testable placements.

Ask five questions and answer each with names, not categories:

1. Which communities, forums, subreddits, and Slack or Discord groups do they participate in?
2. Which newsletters and publications do they actually read?
3. Which events and conferences do they attend?
4. Which podcasts and creators do they follow?
5. Which tools do they already use daily — and do those tools have integration directories or marketplaces?

Then decide **how to show up**: as a genuine participant, as a sponsor, as a contributor of something useful, or through a partnership. The one approach that reliably fails is arriving as an advertiser in a community that did not invite you — the reputational cost outlasts the campaign.

Practical technique: ask ten of your best customers where they learned about tools like yours, and what they read. Ten answers usually name three or four places you had not considered, and the answers come with an implicit endorsement you can act on.

## 5. From raw idea to testable candidate

Most ideas arrive unusable. Convert each before it enters the backlog:

| Raw idea | Testable candidate |
|----------|--------------------|
| "We should do TikTok" | "Post 12 short videos in 4 weeks answering the top 3 support questions; measure profile→site clicks and signups against a €0 baseline" |
| "Improve onboarding" | "Add a 3-step checklist with progress; measure signup→activation over 2 weeks" |
| "Do a referral programme" | "Offer a double-sided credit to the 500 most active users; measure invites sent per user and invite conversion over 30 days" |

The conversion requires a specific change, a metric, a population, and a duration. An idea that cannot be written this way is not ready — either it is too vague, or it is a strategy rather than an experiment.

Cluster the pool before ranking: near-duplicates merge, and clusters reveal themes that are worth a structural bet rather than five separate small tests.

## 6. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Consequence |
|--------------|-------------|
| Only ever using the channels the team already knows | The best channel is never discovered |
| Open group brainstorming with no structure | Anchoring; few ideas; the senior view wins |
| Critiquing during ideation | Bold ideas die before they are understood |
| Ideation with no data input | Ideas about problems you do not have |
| Never reading cancellation reasons or support logs | Ignoring the cheapest, most honest source |
| Copying a competitor's tactic without its context | Inherited constraints without the assets |
| Ideas entering the backlog unconverted | Unrankable, untestable, they rot |
| Marketing in a community as an outsider | Reputational damage that outlives the campaign |
| Confusing an idea pool with a plan | Motion without a thesis (`growth-strategy`) |
| Only additive ideas, never subtractive | Complexity accumulates; the easiest wins are missed |

## 7. Checklist

- [ ] All nineteen channels reviewed and scored, not just the familiar ones
- [ ] Three or four unused channels selected for a cheap test
- [ ] Own data mined first: funnel drop-offs, site search, support, cancellations, reviews
- [ ] Retained-versus-churned cohort differences examined for mechanism
- [ ] A structured silent method (6-3-5 or brainswarming) used instead of open brainstorming
- [ ] Critique separated in time from idea generation
- [ ] Subtractive ideas ("eliminate") explicitly explored
- [ ] Adjacent industries mined for transplantable mechanics
- [ ] Waterholes listed by name, with a decided way of showing up
- [ ] Ten customers asked where they discover tools like yours
- [ ] Every idea converted to change + metric + population + duration
- [ ] Ideas clustered and deduplicated before prioritisation
- [ ] Idea pool fed back into the strategy's current constraint
