# Referral and word of mouth

Goal of this skill: turn existing customers into a repeatable acquisition source — through product mechanics, not only through a rewards page nobody visits.

Use this skill when acquisition costs are rising, when customers are satisfied but quiet, or when building a compounding loop (`growth-loops`).

Prerequisite: people must actually like the product. A referral programme on a mediocre product accelerates negative word of mouth and buys fraud.

---

## 1. Three different mechanisms

| Mechanism | Motivation | Where it lives |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|
| **Word of mouth** | The product is worth talking about | Outside your control; earned |
| **Sharing** | Sharing is part of using the product — a document, an invitation, a result | Built into the product |
| **Referral programme** | An incentive rewards bringing someone | A programme you design and run |

Their relative power is usually in that order, and teams usually work on them in the reverse order. Before building a rewards scheme, ask: **is there a natural moment where sharing helps the user do what they came to do?** Sharing built into the workflow outperforms an incentive scheme because it requires no altruism and no memory.

---

## 2. When a referral programme fits

Good conditions: high satisfaction (measurable as a flattened retention curve and unprompted recommendations); a product whose value is easy to explain in one sentence; users who know other people with the same problem; and a margin that can absorb the reward.

Poor conditions: low retention (you would be paying to accelerate churn); a product people are reluctant to be associated with; a very small or non-overlapping customer network; enterprise sales where the buying committee makes individual referral marginal; and regulated sectors where incentives may be restricted.

The honest check before building: ask thirty recent customers whether they have recommended you, and to whom. If almost nobody has, an incentive will not manufacture the enthusiasm — fix the product or the value proposition first.

---

## 3. Designing the incentive

| Choice | Guidance |
|--------|----------|
| **One-sided or double-sided** | Double-sided (both parties benefit) usually converts better and feels less transactional; it also gives the referrer a reason to talk that is not about themselves |
| **Reward type** | Product credit and upgrades usually beat cash: cheaper for you, more relevant, and less attractive to fraud |
| **Reward size** | Anchored to CAC in the same channel — if paid acquisition costs €40, a €15 double-sided reward is defensible |
| **Trigger point** | Reward on a **qualified** event (activation or first payment), never on signup — rewarding signups is an invitation to fraud |
| **Timing** | Ask at a moment of realised value, not at signup |
| **Limits** | Cap rewards per account and per period |
| **Clarity** | State the terms in one sentence; complexity kills participation |

Reward on signup and you will get fake accounts within days. Reward on a qualified event and the same fraud attempt costs the fraudster real effort for no gain.

Fraud controls worth having from day one: no self-referral (device, payment method, and address checks), qualified-event triggers, per-account caps, a holding period before payout, monitoring of referral clusters, and clear terms allowing you to withhold rewards for abuse. Assume any incentive above trivial value will be attacked.

---

## 4. Mechanics and placement

Placement determines participation far more than reward size. A referral page in account settings reaches almost nobody.

Ask at the moment of realised value: after a successful outcome, at a milestone, after positive feedback or a good review, or when a natural sharing need arises (inviting a collaborator, sending a result).

Reduce friction to the minimum: pre-written message the user can edit, one-click share to the channels they actually use, a personal link that works everywhere, and — importantly — a **landing experience for the invitee that reflects the referral**, naming the referrer and the offer. Sending referred visitors to a generic homepage discards most of the trust the referral created.

Then close the loop: tell the referrer what happened ("Ana signed up"), because visible progress drives repeat referrals more than the reward itself does.

## 5. Measuring

| Metric | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **Participation rate** | Share of eligible users who send at least one invite |
| **Invites per participant** | Depth of sharing |
| **Invite conversion rate** | Share of invitees who become users |
| **k-factor** | Participation × invites × conversion (`growth-loops`) |
| **Cycle time** | Time from referral sent to new user active |
| **Referred-user retention** | Compared against other channels |
| **Cost per referred customer** | Full reward cost, both sides, including fraud losses |
| **Fraud rate** | Rewards withheld or reversed |

Two findings that recur: **referred users usually retain better** than paid-channel users, which means a referral programme can justify a higher cost per acquisition than its CPA suggests; and **participation rate is usually the binding constraint**, not conversion — most people never see or never act on the invitation, which makes placement and timing the highest-leverage variables.

Report k honestly against 1 (`growth-loops`): a k of 0.3 is valuable as a multiplier and is not virality.

## 6. Viral mechanics in the product

Stronger than any programme, because they require no incentive and no memory:

| Mechanic | Example shape |
|----------|---------------|
| **Collaboration** | The product is more useful with others, so users invite colleagues to get value |
| **Shared artefact** | Output is naturally shared and carries an attribution or a route back |
| **Embed** | Content placed on other sites brings visitors back |
| **Communication** | Using the product means contacting a non-user |
| **Public profile or result** | Users publish something that represents them |
| **Network requirement** | The product does not work alone |

Design principles: the sharing must **help the user**, not only the company; keep the recipient experience excellent, because they are a first-time visitor arriving with borrowed trust; never send messages on a user's behalf without explicit, informed consent; and never harvest an address book without clear permission — contact-list scraping is both a data-protection violation in the EU and a reliable way to destroy trust.

## 7. Influencers, creators, and advocates

| Type | Use |
|------|-----|
| **Customer advocates** | Case studies, reviews, testimonials — cheapest and most credible |
| **Niche creators** | Small, engaged, relevant audiences; usually better return than large reach |
| **Large influencers** | Awareness at scale, hard to attribute, expensive |
| **Affiliates** | Performance-based, needs fraud monitoring and brand rules |
| **Partners** | Complementary products, co-marketing, integrations |

Practical guidance: **relevance and engagement beat follower count**; give creators genuine product access and creative freedom rather than a script, since audiences detect and punish scripted endorsement; agree what may not be claimed; measure with unique links or codes, accepting that attribution is imperfect; and start with a small paid test before a large commitment.

**Disclosure is a legal requirement, not a courtesy.** Paid or incentivised endorsements — including free products and affiliate links — must be clearly labelled as advertising. In Germany and the EU, undisclosed advertising is unfair competition and can be pursued by competitors and consumer associations, with the liability sitting on both the creator and the commissioning brand. Put disclosure obligations in the contract and check compliance (`growth-legal-and-ethics`).

The genuine risk: an influencer's reputation becomes attached to yours. Vet, and keep an exit clause.

## 8. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Consequence |
|--------------|-------------|
| Referral programme on a product with poor retention | Paying to accelerate churn; amplifying negative word of mouth |
| Rewarding signups rather than qualified events | Fraud within days |
| Cash rewards without fraud controls | Organised abuse; budget converted into fake accounts |
| Referral link only in account settings | Nobody sees it; participation near zero |
| Asking at signup rather than after value | No basis for a recommendation yet |
| Referred visitors landing on a generic homepage | The trust from the referral is discarded |
| Referrer never told what happened | No repeat referrals |
| Complex, multi-tier terms | Participation collapses |
| Sending messages on users' behalf without consent | Spam complaints, data-protection breach |
| Address-book harvesting | GDPR violation and reputational damage |
| Undisclosed paid endorsement | Unfair-competition liability for brand and creator |
| Follower count as the selection criterion | Expensive reach with no conversion |
| Reporting k below 1 as "viral" | Forecasts that never arrive |

---

## 9. Checklist

- [ ] Retention and satisfaction verified before building a referral programme
- [ ] Natural in-product sharing moments identified and built before an incentive scheme
- [ ] Incentive structure chosen (one- or double-sided), sized against CAC
- [ ] Reward triggered by a qualified event, never by signup
- [ ] Fraud controls in place: self-referral checks, caps, holding period, cluster monitoring
- [ ] Terms stated in one sentence
- [ ] Ask placed at a moment of realised value, not in settings
- [ ] Friction minimised: pre-written message, one-click share, personal link
- [ ] Invitee landing experience reflects the referral and the referrer
- [ ] Referrer notified of outcomes
- [ ] Participation, invites, conversion, k-factor, and cycle time measured
- [ ] Referred-user retention compared against other channels
- [ ] Fraud rate and full reward cost included in cost per referred customer
- [ ] No messages sent or contacts harvested without explicit consent
- [ ] Creator partnerships selected on relevance; disclosure obligations contracted and verified
