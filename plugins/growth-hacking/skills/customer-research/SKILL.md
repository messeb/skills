# Customer research for growth

Goal of this skill: know who to reach, what they are trying to do, what they use today, and the words they use — because every channel, message, and onboarding decision depends on it.

Use this skill before defining a target group, when conversion is unexplained, when messaging tests all perform the same, or when the team argues about "the user" without evidence.

---

## 1. Finding people to talk to

The most common blocker is "we have no users yet". Ways through it:

| Situation | Approach |
|-----------|----------|
| No product, no users | Find people with the problem in waterholes (`idea-generation`) — communities, forums, events |
| Some users | Talk to the most recent signups (memory is fresh) and the most engaged |
| Churned users | The highest-value and least-contacted group; offer a small incentive |
| B2B, no customers | Your network, second-degree introductions, targeted outreach with a research framing, not a sales framing |
| Competitor's customers | Review sites, communities, one-star reviews — and ask them directly |
| Nobody responds | Your ask is too big or too vague: request 15 minutes about a specific problem, not "feedback on our product" |

Aim for **8–12 conversations per segment**. Patterns stabilise around eight; beyond fifteen you are usually confirming what you already know.

## 2. Interviewing without generating false positives

The failure mode is friendly, useless data: people are polite, they predict their behaviour badly, and they will validate almost any idea if asked directly.

| Ask | Not |
|-----|-----|
| "Tell me about the last time you had to do X." | "Would you use a product that does X?" |
| "What did you do about it?" | "Do you think this is a good idea?" |
| "What did that cost you — time, money, stress?" | "How much would you pay?" |
| "What have you tried before? Why did you stop?" | "Would you switch?" |
| "Walk me through what happened that day." | "How often do you generally do this?" |
| "What did you type into the search box?" | "How would you find something like this?" |

The rules: **ask about the past, not the future**; ask about specific instances, not general habits; look for **evidence of prior effort** — money spent, a workaround built, a spreadsheet maintained — because that is the only reliable proof a problem is real; and never pitch during the interview, because everything after the pitch is contaminated.

Silence is the most productive technique. After an answer, wait three seconds; the second half is usually the honest half.

### The switching story

For anyone who recently adopted or abandoned something, reconstruct the timeline: when they first noticed the problem, what triggered active looking, what they compared, who else was involved in the decision, what almost stopped them, and what they expected on day one. This surfaces the four forces that determine adoption — the **push** of the current situation, the **pull** of the new option, the **anxiety** about switching, and the **habit** holding them in place. Each maps directly to a growth intervention: amplify push and pull in messaging, reduce anxiety with guarantees and proof, break habit with migration help and defaults.

## 3. Segmentation that is actually useful

Demographics are the least predictive way to segment a software audience. Segment by something that changes what people do:

| Basis | Example | Useful for |
|-------|---------|-----------|
| **Situation / trigger** | "Just hired their first employee" | Timing, targeting, messaging |
| **Job to be done** | "Needs to prove compliance to an auditor" | Positioning, feature priority |
| **Behaviour** | "Uses the export feature weekly" | Activation and expansion |
| **Value / potential** | Top 10% by contribution margin | Where to spend acquisition budget |
| **Acquisition source** | Arrived via comparison content | Channel-specific onboarding |
| **Firmographics (B2B)** | Size, industry, tooling | Sales motion and channel fit |
| Demographics | Age, gender, location | Rarely predictive on its own; useful for ad targeting mechanics |

The two segments worth identifying first: **who retains best** (find more of them) and **who churns fastest** (stop acquiring them). Both are computable from existing data, and the second regularly reveals that a "cheap" channel is buying customers who leave.

## 4. Personas — evidenced or labelled as assumptions

A persona built in a workshop from team knowledge is a **proto-persona**: useful for alignment, dangerous when cited as fact. Build it fast, mark every field as `assumed` or `evidenced`, and turn the risky assumptions into research questions.

Keep personas **behavioural**: their situation, what they are trying to achieve, what they use today, their constraints, how they decide, and where they look for solutions. Age and job title rarely change a design decision; "evaluates tools by trying them before contacting sales" changes several.

Cap at three to five, name the primary one, date them, and re-validate on a schedule. An undated persona is treated as permanently true.

Discovery methods overlap heavily with product discovery. Rather than duplicating them, use the dedicated skills where the work goes deeper:

| Need | Skill |
|------|-------|
| Full design-thinking empathize/define process, personas, empathy maps, journey maps, POV and How Might We framing | `product-discovery:design-thinking` |
| Jobs-to-be-done interviews, the four forces, job maps, opportunity scoring | `product-discovery:jobs-to-be-done` |

| Surveys at scale, sampling, wording, standard instruments | `product-discovery:questionnaires` |
| Stakeholder mapping and interview guides | `product-discovery:stakeholder-interviews` |

What this skill adds on top is the **growth** framing: research is only finished when it has produced a testable hypothesis with a metric attached (§7).

## 6. Synthesis artefacts

**Empathy map** — for one persona in one specific situation: what they say (verbatim), do (observed), think and feel (inferred, and marked as inferred), plus pains and gains. Fast, and forces you to separate quotes from interpretation.

**Journey map** — stages across the whole experience, including the parts you do not own: what they are trying to achieve, what they actually do, which touchpoints are involved, their emotional state with a reason for each dip, the pain points with frequency and severity, and who owns each stage today.

The most valuable output of a journey map is usually the **dip nobody owns** — the stage every team assumed was another team's responsibility. In growth terms that is normally the highest-leverage intervention available.

Keep the whole exercise proportionate: these are synthesis formats for research you have done, not substitutes for doing it.

## 7. From research to growth hypotheses

Research that does not change what you test was expensive entertainment. Convert systematically:

| Finding | Growth hypothesis |
|---------|-------------------|
| "I searched for a comparison with X and only found their page" | Publish a comparison page; expect branded-comparison traffic to convert above baseline |
| "I did not trust it with our client data" | Add security proof above the fold; expect signup rate to rise for enterprise-domain visitors |
| "I only understood the value after my colleague showed me" | Multiplayer onboarding: prompt to invite a colleague at activation; expect week-4 retention to rise |
| "I was already using a spreadsheet for this" | Target spreadsheet-workaround language in ads and content; expect a lower cost per qualified signup |
| Churned users cite "never got it set up" | Reduce time-to-value; expect week-1 retention to rise |

Two things to carry forward verbatim: the **words customers use** become your ad copy, page headlines, and search keywords — they will not search for your internal product vocabulary. And the **alternative they name**, including "a spreadsheet" and "nothing", is your real competition and belongs in your positioning.

## 8. Anti-patterns

| Anti-pattern | Consequence |
|--------------|-------------|
| Asking whether people would use it | Polite yes, no purchase |
| Pitching during the interview | Everything afterwards is contaminated |
| Talking only to happy current users | Survivorship bias; churn stays unexplained |
| Demographic segmentation for software | Segments that do not predict behaviour |
| Workshop personas cited as research | Confident decisions on fiction |
| Personas never dated or re-validated | Quietly wrong, still on the wall |
| Research done, artefacts made, nothing tested | Expensive documentation |
| Product vocabulary used in ads and content | Nobody searches for your internal terms |
| Ignoring "nothing" and "a spreadsheet" as competitors | Positioning against the wrong alternative |
| Interviewing without recording the exact words | The most reusable output is lost |
| Skipping churned users | The most informative group never heard |

---

## 9. Checklist

- [ ] 8–12 conversations per segment, including churned and non-adopting people
- [ ] Questions about past behaviour and specific instances, never predictions
- [ ] Evidence of prior effort sought as proof the problem is real
- [ ] No pitching during research conversations
- [ ] Switching timeline reconstructed for recent adopters and leavers
- [ ] Four forces (push, pull, anxiety, habit) documented per segment
- [ ] Segmentation based on situation, job, behaviour, or value — not demographics alone
- [ ] Best-retaining and fastest-churning segments identified from data
- [ ] Personas marked assumed versus evidenced, capped at five, dated
- [ ] Inferences in empathy maps explicitly marked as inferences
- [ ] Journey map covers stages outside your own product; unowned dips identified
- [ ] Verbatim customer language captured for copy and keywords
- [ ] The named alternative (including "nothing") recorded as the real competitor
- [ ] Every significant finding converted into a testable growth hypothesis
